What People Have Criticized Mayor Ken Kincade For — and What the Record Shows

Ken Kincade has been Cabot's mayor since January 1, 2019, and is seeking a third term in the November 3, 2026 election against former mayor Eddie Joe Williams. This page catalogues the recurring criticisms of his tenure — from his challenger, from council members, from residents at public hearings, and from the local Facebook groups — and pairs each one with what the documentary record actually establishes and with the answers Kincade and his administration have given on the record. Criticisms are attributed to the people who made them; this page reaches no verdict of its own.

The story

Kincade, a former Cabot firefighter, was first elected in 2018. His tenure has coincided with rapid growth: the $32 million Hwy. 67/167 interchange opened in 2019, the Game Time at the Grounds sports complex followed, and a long list of chain restaurants and stores opened along the city's corridors. Four threads of criticism run through the record of those years:

  • - 2021 — the money year. The council created an economic development incentive fund (Ordinance No. 35 of 2021), under which the mayor could authorize incentive payments up to $35,000. In August 2021, Cabot voters approved a $69 million bond issue in a special election, funded by continuing the city's one-cent sales tax (first approved in 2005, extended in 2013). The city later refinanced the bonds from 5% to 3% while taking on additional debt at the lower rate.
  • - January–February 2022 — Flock arrives. The city signed a lease contract with Flock Safety in January 2022, after testing the cameras for about three months; installation began in February. Nine cameras eventually operated. The service appeared as a line item in the 2024, 2025, and 2026 budgets rather than as a standalone public decision.
  • - March 2024 — the incentive check. The city paid $34,900 from its community and economic development incentive fund to EJ Holdings, the Fayetteville-based developer of the Olive Garden property, toward demolishing an existing building on the site. The payment was recorded in the treasurer's general ledger and appeared in an October 2024 program presentation, but it was not on the list of incentive payments the economic development director provided to the council in April 2025.
  • - September 2025 — the FOIA fight. Council Member Brandon Hillenburg, who had filed a Freedom of Information Act request in May 2025 and had not received the check in the response, raised the payment at a council meeting and at first took it for an illicit payment. Kincade publicly blasted council members for filing FOIA requests instead of calling him, and Hillenburg and Maggie Cope pushed back on the tone. The council's response was an ordinance amendment — given first and second readings that September — to require council approval of incentive requests above $5,000.
  • - 2026 — the cameras come down. In January 2026, Council Member Corey Spangler emailed the mayor suggesting the city look at its Flock policies; the mayor, police chief, and city attorney began a review. On August 6, 2026, Kincade ordered all nine cameras covered and disconnected. The council debated the program on August 17, held a two-hour public hearing on October 19, and that night a resolution to permanently remove the cameras and poles failed 2–3–3 (Hillenburg and Kyle Pickard yes; Cope, Stephen Redd, and James Reid no; Ed Long, Spangler, and Matt Webber abstaining). The cameras remain covered, neither reinstated nor permanently removed.
  • - Fall 2026 — the campaign frame. Williams built his challenge around traffic, debt, and jobs. At the Chamber of Commerce forum reported October 14, Kincade answered with growth: doubled sales-tax revenue, reserves, and bonds being retired early. On October 28, the Leader's front page took up the debt question directly and examined both campaigns' fundraising.

What critics said

The debt, and how it was approved

This is Williams's central charge. Citing the legislative audit, he says the city carries nearly $100 million in debt and warns the obligations stretch far into the future. His sharper point is about consent: reviewing the 2021 special-election returns, he notes that of roughly 17,000 registered voters, only about 900 cast ballots and about 700 voted yes — a large majority of those who voted, but a small slice of the city committing it to long-term payments. He says he fought low-turnout special elections as a state senator and argues major financial decisions deserve broader participation and awareness.

Growth, traffic, and roads

The most persistent resident complaint is not ideological: it is traffic. In the Facebook groups, discussion of the mayor is overwhelmingly complaint-driven — congestion, construction that seems to move from one mess to another, drainage ditches, school-zone speeding, and skepticism that the interchange work will actually relieve cross-town delays. One widely engaged BCOC post in August 2026 blamed Kincade personally for interchange spending and traffic delays; commenters split between blaming him, noting the road problems predated him by decades, and defending his record. Williams's campaign echoes the theme, arguing growth without good management creates chaos and pointing to residents who drive to Beebe or Jacksonville to shop to avoid Cabot congestion.

The Flock cameras — from both directions

Kincade has been criticized over the cameras coming in, and over how they went off:

  • - Privacy critics — including council candidate Courtney Roldan and former state Rep. R.D. Hopper at the October 19 hearing — describe the system as mass surveillance: a private company collecting plate data to cloud servers, shared with other agencies, building a searchable record of where law-abiding residents travel. Roldan argued citizens should never have to justify wanting to be left alone.
  • - Process critics note the cameras were never the subject of a standalone council vote the public could weigh in on beforehand; Council Member Matt Webber observed at the August 17 meeting that the council approved Flock only as a budget line item. The August 26 Leader also reported sharp resident criticism at an area council meeting over Flock's data-sharing and a company video shown to a council — in that instance a neighboring city's meeting, but the same regional argument playing out in Cabot's hearing room.
  • - Critics of the suspension itself — including retired law-enforcement voices at the August 17 debate — argued that if no Cabot officer had misused the system, suspending it punished good officers and helped only "bad actors and their lawyers." Council Member Maggie Cope pressed the mayor directly: if nobody in the department misused it, what was the final straw?

Transparency and the incentive program

The September 2025 FOIA episode is the concrete case. Hillenburg's complaint was twofold: a $34,900 incentive payment he could not find on the administration's own list to the council, and a FOIA response that did not include the check. Kincade's response at the meeting — criticizing members for going "behind my back" with FOIA requests instead of phoning him — drew its own criticism from Cope, who said the exchange showed the council could not have a civil conversation. In the 2026 campaign the theme has been picked up by others: Roldan proposes publishing council packets a week ahead instead of the Friday before and posting a city budget summary on the website, saying transparency is lacking; Williams pledges transparency in city government. Critics of the incentive program also note the structure itself: the mayor could approve up to $35,000 alone, and the EJ Holdings check, at $34,900, sat just under that cap.

Development deals generally

Beyond the one check, the administration's growth model — incentive payments to recruit businesses, land sales and long leases for projects like the Lyon College veterinary school and the Buckman Brewhouse lease of the old downtown fire station — draws recurring questions in coverage and conversation about who benefits and on what terms. (The Leader's August 12, 2026 profile of Kincade reports the council approved $263,500 in incentives for larger recruits, including $88,500 toward Chick-fil-A, and $179,961 in Move Cabot Forward awards to 36 small businesses. Dollar figures attached to the Buckman Brewhouse arrangements conflict across sources and are not treated as settled here.)

In the Facebook groups

Mined as a primary source (themes only, paraphrased; see Sources), the groups add texture rather than new factual claims: Flock was the dominant civic topic of August–October 2026, sharply polarized between surveillance framing and public-safety framing, with a later turn toward residents demanding cameras in places that lacked them (a vandalized park, October 24). Mayor-focused discussion was mostly about roads, drainage, and speeding rather than the election itself; a December 2025 post circulated officials' phone numbers urging residents to call and demand action on a grievance. Fresh searches on October 30, 2026 for "Kincade," "Williams," and "debt" in the three largest groups surfaced little substantive mayoral-race or debt discussion — and much of BCOC's mayor content is image-based and cannot be characterized from text.

What the record shows

  • - The debt total is real; the characterization is contested. The Leader (Oct. 28, 2026) puts Cabot's total outstanding bonded debt at the end of 2022 at approximately $99.65 million — consistent with Williams's "nearly $100 million" — while noting much of it is backed by dedicated revenue sources. The 2021 portion was voter-approved, is funded by the continuing one-cent sales tax rather than a new tax, and was refinanced from 5% to 3%. The city's position, stated in the same Oct. 28 coverage, is that the 35-year bonds can be paid off in 17 years even with no growth; at the Oct. 14 forum Kincade put the payoff in 2038 — 18 years early — and stressed the debt is collateralized by the sales tax, not a general obligation of city funds. The Leader's own assessment was that there is no evidence the bond issues have hurt the city's finances. It is also unclear, the paper noted, whether Williams publicly opposed the 2021 bond issue before that election.
  • - The turnout point checks out as Williams states it. The ~900 ballots / ~700 yes votes / ~17,000 registered voters figures are his, published in the Oct. 28 Leader; the same article confirms the measure passed "overwhelmingly" among those who voted. Whether a low-turnout special election is a legitimate way to approve long-term debt is a judgment the sources leave to the reader.
  • - On Flock, the timeline is documented. Contract January 2022; installation February 2022; budgeted 2024–2026; the police department's reported results for January–June 2026 were 8.04 million plates scanned, 688 matches against crime databases, seven stolen vehicles recovered, and 19 arrests; two retention clocks applied — Flock's system deleted footage on a rolling 30-day basis (the window the department had to extract anything it meant to keep), while plate data the department itself preserved was capped at 150 days under its 2022 policy, longer only for an ongoing investigation; outside-agency sharing was suspended before the cameras were covered. The suspension was the mayor's own order (Aug. 6, 2026), and his stated reasons were privacy concerns after further reading on the national debate and reports of improper uses elsewhere — while saying he did not believe Cabot's police had misused it. Kincade also told the council that in the past year only one person, an out-of-state emailer, had contacted him about the cameras before Spangler's January 2026 email prompted the review. As of late October 2026 the question is unresolved by design: the council declined to remove the cameras permanently (2–3–3) and has not reinstated them; a review committee process and Resolution No. 34 of 2026 set criteria for the suspension period. Separately, Spangler introduced an ordinance (No. 33 of 2026) to bar city funds for third-party plate-reader systems; it had a first reading October 19 and no recorded vote in the minutes available.
  • - The FOIA episode: the paper trail supports the council member's narrower point. The $34,900 EJ Holdings payment existed in the treasurer's ledger and in an October 2024 presentation, but was missing from the list provided to the council in April 2025 — an omission the Leader documented. Nothing in the coverage shows the payment itself was unlawful; it was within the mayor's $35,000 authority under the 2021 program, and Hillenburg's initial suspicion of an illicit payment was, in the paper's telling, a mistake. The council's institutional answer — requiring its approval for incentives above $5,000 — received first and second readings in September 2025; whether the amendment was finally adopted could not be confirmed from the sources reviewed for this page.
  • - The growth record is also documented. The business openings the administration lists (Chick-fil-A, Olive Garden, Whataburger, Slim Chickens, IHOP, hotels, and others) are matters of public record, as are Game Time at the Grounds and the Lyon College vet school project. Kincade's forum claim that sales-tax revenue has doubled in eight years is his claim as reported; it was not independently verified in the sources reviewed. That residents experience the same growth as congestion is equally documented in the groups and in the forum answers of nearly every candidate.
  • - What remains unknown: the Democrat-Gazette has published no dedicated article on the 2026 Cabot mayoral race (its Voter Guide lists the race), so there is no second newspaper's assessment of these criticisms; the final status of the incentive-approval amendment and of the Flock program were both open when the sources for this page were compiled (late October 2026).
