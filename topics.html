Topics

Some Cabot stories get asked about again and again. Each of these pages tells one of them the same way: the story itself, with dates; what critics and residents said, including the Facebook group conversation paraphrased as themes; and what the record actually shows — including the parts nobody has documented, which are named as gaps rather than filled in.

  • Olive Garden & the Park That Hasn't Happened — A restaurant, a promised mountain-bike park, a sewer line the city ran anyway, and a refund fight over ratepayer money.
  • Buckman Brewhouse — The brewery in the old downtown fire station: two tie-break votes, a permit fight, and lease figures that do not agree across sources.
  • Water & Sewer Rate Increases — How rate charts from 2001 and 2005 gave way to the increases of 2025–2026, what drove them, and what residents said.
  • Why Doug Warner Resigned — The 2020 council resignation, in Warner's own stated reasons, and the runoff coda most people forget.
  • Criticism of Mayor Kincade — The recurring criticisms of Mayor Ken Kincade's tenure — debt, growth, Flock, transparency — paired with his answers and what the record shows.
  • What Eddie Joe Williams Is Running On — His 2026 platform — traffic, debt, jobs, transparency — the record he points to, and the scrutiny his campaign has drawn.
  • The Flock Camera Fight — Nine plate-reader cameras ran for four years with almost no debate, then became the city's biggest argument: the suspension, the 2–3–3 vote, and the Facebook discourse that drove it.
  • The Crypto Mine and Act 314 — A crypto mine in the county, a council that extended its rules to reach it at its senator's urging — and the state law, passed with his vote five weeks later, that took the authority away.
  • Growth, Traffic, and the Interchanges — Cabot's defining grievance: the $32M north interchange, the $53.9M Hwy. 5 rebuild, who actually owns which roads, and what the Facebook groups say about all of it.
  • The Lyon College Deal: A Veterinary School for Cabot — The biggest development deal in city history — a land sale, a 25-year lease-and-sublease, a developer swap nobody explained, and a discourse that never happened.
  • The Junior High North Fire of 2006 — Registration day, August 10, 2006: one of the district's newest schools burned to the ground. The response, the $12.1 million settlement, the three-year rebuild — and the anniversary nobody marks.
  • What the Bond Money Built — Two decades of a one-cent sales tax and what it bought: Game Time, the fiber network, the Justice Center, the sirens — the inventory, the separate money pots, and the debt argument at the center of the 2026 race.
  • The Tornado of March 29, 1976 — An F3 tornado leveled downtown on a Monday afternoon in 1976, killing five. The rebuilding on the rubble, the festival born from it, and the siren argument Cabot is still having fifty years later.
  • The City That Built Its Own Internet — Fed up with the cable monopoly, Cabot built its own fiber network as a public utility and leased it to an electric co-op subsidiary — the most popular item in the 2021 bond package, and the least examined since.
  • First Street: The Railroad Town the Tornado Took — The Cabot that grew up around the railroad stop — Old Austin left behind, cotton wagons and general stores, First Street along the tracks — and the downtown that stood there until one Monday afternoon in 1976.
  • The Amphitheater Fight of 2026 — A $50,000 study for a possible $10–15 million amphitheater, one of the year's largest Facebook threads, and a public correction three days later — a fight about money pots, and about how Cabot finds out what its government is doing.
  • Old Austin: The Town the Railroad Left Behind — The Butterfield stop the railroad passed by: Austin's antebellum town, Camp Nelson's 1,500 dead and their disputed move, the Sears House, and the college that almost came in 1886.
  • The Therapy Pool: The Summer Cabot Organized for a Repair — A 20-year-old dehumidifier died in January 2026 and the therapy pool sat closed for eight months — until residents organized, three pots of money split a $129,825 repair, and the council voted unanimously.
