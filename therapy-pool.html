The Therapy Pool: The Summer Cabot Organized for a Repair

In January 2026, the heated therapy pool at Cabot's Veterans Park Community Center went dark. The dehumidification system that made a 92-to-95-degree indoor pool possible had failed at the end of its expected life, and the Cabot Parks and Recreation Commission — which runs the center and is separate from the city — could not pay to replace it on its own. The pool stayed closed for roughly eight months while the funding was assembled piece by piece: a resident campaign in the Facebook groups, a July appearance before the Advertising and Promotion Commission, and an August 17, 2026 city council vote that split the $129,825 cost three ways. The episode became the clearest statement of the counter-argument running underneath Cabot's build-new era: that the facilities the city already has — and the seniors and disabled residents who use them daily — deserve the same urgency as the ones being built.

The story

The building. The Veterans Park Community Center, at 508 N. Lincoln Street across from Cabot High School, was built in 2006 and houses the Parks and Recreation Department's administrative offices (Cabot Parks and Recreation). Inside are a 25-yard indoor heated pool, basketball courts, an indoor walking track, weight and fitness rooms, a racquetball court, and the therapy pool; the center also contains a community tornado safe room, and the Veterans Park Event Center stands in the same complex. The city's 2023 State of the City address described the community center as a wellness staple with more than 4,000 members and 3,600 fitness classes offered in 2022. It is the workhorse of the parks system — the facility the 2013 and 2021 bond programs built around, with Game Time at the Grounds, the aquatic park expansion, and the turfed complexes rising elsewhere while the 2006 building aged into its repair years.

The pool. The therapy pool is a small, heated pool maintained at roughly 92 to 95 degrees, used for injury prevention and recovery, aquatic fitness, and swim lessons (Cabot Parks and Recreation facility descriptions). Its documented users skew toward the residents with the fewest alternatives: seniors managing arthritis and mobility limits, people in physical rehabilitation — the Leader's August 2026 account included a resident whose pain doctor had directed her to use the therapy pool after physical therapy — and special-needs users who cannot tolerate the main pool's colder water or its four-to-five-foot depth, a point made repeatedly by residents during the closure. Where Game Time was built for tournaments and visitors, the therapy pool's constituency is local, older, and daily.

A repair history before 2026. The 2026 failure was not the pool's first. Parks reports carried in council packets record the therapy pool's motor out of service in June 2020, and by August 2020 its heater had failed after 14 years — against a typical six-year lifespan — with a $3,200 replacement ordered; the same report described air-conditioning and humidity problems elsewhere in the center. Both the heater and the dehumidifier dated to the building's 2006 construction.

The breakdown. In January 2026 the pool's dehumidifier failed and the pool closed. Parks Director Adam Ussery, who had taken over as director that same month, later told the Leader the unit had been installed in 2006 with a 20-year life expectancy. The failure was unanticipated and unbudgeted. Parks first tried to find the money itself, concluded it could not make the numbers work on its own, and went out for quotes (Leader, Aug. 26, 2026). The quote that reached the council packet, dated May 22, 2026, came from Harrison Energy Partners of Little Rock: remove the existing 4-ton PoolPak unit and its ducting, install a new PoolPak dehumidification system and condenser, extend the concrete pad to meet current mechanical-code clearances, and rework the exterior ductwork — $129,825, excluding tax (Harrison Energy Partners proposal PR05831, in the Aug. 17, 2026 council packet).

Assembling the money. In July 2026, Parks took its funding request to the A&P Commission — the same separate tax pot that had committed $900,000 toward the wave pool in January (Leader, Aug. 26, 2026; see the bond-money page). Council Member Maggie Cope noted during the August discussion that A&P had given Parks and Rec a $1.2 million budget for 2026 at the start of the year (Leader, Aug. 26, 2026). The city's share required council action, which is how a parks commission's broken dehumidifier became a council agenda item — and a public campaign.

The vote. On Monday, August 17, 2026, the item moved through both of the evening's sessions. In the work session, Mayor Ken Kincade presented the resolution; discussion covered the pool's condition, the needed repairs, existing service agreements, the maintenance history, estimated costs, and the timeline of the request for city help. Committee Member Matt Webber moved to forward it to the full council, seconded by Brandon Hillenburg, and the motion passed 5–0. In the regular meeting that followed, Resolution 35 of 2026 — authorizing the mayor and city clerk-treasurer to enter an agreement with the Parks and Recreation Commission to support the repairs and amending the General Fund's 2026 budget to provide for it — was read by title only; Stephen Redd moved to adopt, Hillenburg seconded, and it passed unanimously on a roll call vote (Aug. 17, 2026 minutes, in the Sept. 21, 2026 packet). The minutes record no community input on the item at the regular meeting. At the meeting, Council Member Ed Long pressed the timeline — the pool had been closed since January, and he asked why it had taken eight months for Parks to come to the council for help, saying the community's seniors should not have to go without a therapy pool that supports their well-being (Leader, Aug. 26, 2026). The filed resolution's sponsor stamp credits Mayor Kincade and Council Member Corey Spangler as its sponsors (council-record sponsor verification).

The announced plan and the silence after. The Leader's front-page account on August 26, 2026 reported the arrangement: the $129,825 cost split three ways — the City Council, Parks and Rec, and the A&P Commission — at $43,275 each, with Harrison Energy Partners doing the installation and repairs expected to take eight to twelve weeks; the pool, the paper reported, might reopen by the end of the year. After that story, the record in the sources reviewed goes quiet: no Leader follow-up through the October 28, 2026 issue, and no therapy pool item in the September or October council packets in this corpus. Whether the work began on that schedule is not documented here.

What critics said

The criticism in this story ran in two directions at once: at the fact of the closure — a heavily used facility for seniors and disabled residents sat broken for most of a year — and at the priorities it seemed to reveal, in a season when the city was finishing a $16 million indoor sports complex, building a $2.8 million wave pool, and (weeks later) studying a possible $10–15 million amphitheater. Officials' own questions joined the public's: the eight-month timeline was challenged from the council table itself, by Ed Long, at the August 17 meeting (Leader, Aug. 26, 2026).

The Facebook discourse

The organizing lived in Cabot Strong, the largest of the local groups; searches of WTF CABOT ARKANSAS, Cabot Strong For Better Change, and Cabot Strong (uncensored) found no comparable therapy pool campaign (the WTF results were local massage and physical-therapy businesses). It unfolded as a small, purposeful sequence rather than a single viral thread:

  • - The setup question (July 13, 2026; 7 reactions, 9 comments). A resident asked whether the community center is even owned by the city, and what the process would be for the city to fund a repair the post put at over $150,000. Commenters supplied the council directory link, said they had already messaged council members, and urged others to do the same; the themes were that the center is a wonderful place that needs to be kept up, that it is used by older and disabled people, and that there always seems to be money for other things.
  • - The A&P alert (July 19, 2026; 8 reactions, 11 comments). A post told interested residents the funding request would be discussed at the A&P board's Tuesday-morning meeting and urged them to attend and show support. Comments mixed logistics (who sits on the commission) with facility grievance: the building had been remodeled only a couple of years earlier, in one recollection; the roof and pool had been fixed repeatedly since it was built, another said, wondering whether corners were cut in the original construction; the main pool's temperature was inconsistent; the women's dressing room had a single changing room and two showers for large class groups. One commenter asked, in effect, whether a broken facility should not simply already be fixed.
  • - The agenda posts (August 13, 34 reactions and 24 comments; August 14, 32 reactions and 20 comments). With the vote set for Monday, posts quoted the resolution's agenda text, linked the council's contact page, and asked residents to tell the mayor and council the pool mattered. The August 14 post added the suspicion that the council did not believe the pool was useful to the community — and the comparison that framed the whole campaign: the new wave pool was costing $2.8 million.
  • - The follow-up (August 18, 2026; 16 reactions, 4 comments). The morning after the vote, a resident asked the group how it had gone. A reply summarized the three-way split — and described the total cost as around $43,000, which is one party's share rather than the $129,825 whole. Other comments widened the upkeep list, including a call to fix the air conditioning in the community center.

The comments on the August posts were dominated by the users themselves, and their testimony is the core of the discourse:

  • - The pool as medicine. Commenter after commenter described the warm water as treatment, not recreation: relief for arthritis in backs and hips, muscle and mobility problems, fibromyalgia that the cold main pool aggravated. One described using the pool for a long time for arthritis, then — after a January spinal cord injury and a period of paralysis — working back toward walking with therapy, and waiting on the pool's return. Another described working with special-needs people whose goals the therapy pool served because they could not tolerate the main pool's cold or depth. Several said the heated pool was the reason they chose Cabot's center over Jacksonville's or Sherwood's, or the specific reason their family had joined at all — and that the closure made the membership feel wasted.
  • - The weariness of asking. Multiple commenters said they asked staff about the repair every week, sometimes twice a week, and were always told there was no update. Others asked the structural questions: why is it broken, why does a repair require the city council at all, are repairs not already budgeted for?
  • - The new-build contrast, and its pushback. The wave pool comparison ran through the campaign from its first post. Inside the same threads, at least one commenter corrected the frame: the wave pool was a voter-approved bond initiative, not the same pot or process as a repair. A separate comment raised the bond debt itself, asking how far behind the city was on its payments before approving anything new.
  • - Election-season pressure. Some comments named council members directly and tied the vote to the November election — support it or answer for it. The pressure was not abstract: two of the members named in the thread were on the ballot that fall.
  • - Modest scale, by design. The posts drew dozens of reactions, not hundreds — nothing like the amphitheater or Flock threads. The campaign's method was not virality but contact: links to the council directory, meeting times and addresses, and a follow-up question the next morning to close the loop.

What the record shows

  • - What failed. Not the pool itself, but the dehumidification system that served the therapy pool area — a 4-ton PoolPak unit installed in 2006 that reached the end of the 20-year life expectancy its manufacturer-era design carried, according to the parks director as reported by the Leader (Aug. 26, 2026). The replacement scope in the vendor's proposal is mechanical: new PoolPak unit and condenser, new exterior ductwork, a concrete pad extension required by current code clearances, crane work, and factory start-up (Harrison Energy Partners proposal PR05831, May 22, 2026, in the Aug. 17, 2026 council packet).
  • - The cost, and the figures that don't match it. The documented price is $129,825 (excluding tax), appearing in both the vendor proposal in the packet and the Leader's reporting. The three-way split and the $43,275 shares are from the Leader's August 26 account; the text of Resolution 35 itself — including any dollar figure in the agreement it authorized — does not appear in the council packets in this corpus, so the shares rest on the paper's reporting rather than the resolution's language. Two other figures circulated in the groups and match nothing in the documents: a pre-vote resident estimate of "over $150,000," and a post-vote description of the cost as "around $43,000," which corresponds to a single party's share.
  • - Why it took three payers. The center belongs to the Parks and Recreation Commission's operation, not a city department's budget — the fact underneath the campaign's opening question about who owns the building. Parks' account, via the Leader, is that it first tried to fund the replacement itself, ran its own financials, got vendor quotes, and only then took the request to the A&P Commission in July and the council in August. That sequence is the documented answer to the eight-month question, though the record does not show why the quote obtained in May became a funding request in July rather than sooner.
  • - The A&P third is the least documented step. This corpus holds no A&P Commission minutes. A resident post on August 18 said the final third was to go before the A&P Commission that day; the Leader's August 26 story describes the three-way split as the arrangement. The commission's own vote — its date and terms — is a gap in the record assembled here.
  • - What the council actually did. Resolution 35 of 2026 authorized an agreement with the Parks and Recreation Commission "to support repairs" and amended the 2026 General Fund budget to provide for it; it was adopted unanimously after a 5–0 committee referral the same evening (Aug. 17, 2026 minutes). The minutes' record of the regular meeting shows no public input on the item — the residents' campaign is documented in the groups and in the Leader's interviews, not in the minutes.
  • - The repair's status is unknown in these sources. The Leader's 8-to-12-week estimate and end-of-year reopening possibility (Aug. 26, 2026) are the last word in the corpus: no subsequent Leader issue through October 28, 2026, and no September or October council packet here, reports on the installation or the pool's reopening.
