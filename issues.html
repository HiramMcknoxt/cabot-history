Cabot's Local Issues, Explained

This page tracks the arguments Cabot is actually having with itself — the ones running through council meetings, the Arkansas Leader, and the city's Facebook groups — and tries to do three things with each: explain what the issue is in plain English, lay out the arguments on all sides the way the people making them would recognize, and check the specific claims circulating locally against the record this Repository is built on. Where the record is thin, the page says so rather than filling the gap.

The resident-discussion material comes from read-only surveys of Cabot's public-facing Facebook groups (Cabot Strong, WTF CABOT ARKANSAS, Cabot's Petty Citizens – BCOC, and smaller groups), October–November 2026, plus the council packets, minutes, and Leader issues in the archive. Discussion is paraphrased into themes; no resident post or comment is quoted, and no private resident is named. Group sentiment is qualitative — it shows what the argument sounds like, never how many Cabot residents hold a view. Nothing on this page is a poll result.

The Repository polls

Starting with the November 16, 2026 council meeting, the Repository runs a poll on every city council agenda. On the Thursday before each regular meeting, the meeting's agenda is published with a plain-English summary of every item and a poll asking readers how they would vote on each one. After the meeting, the poll results are posted here alongside what the council actually did — a running record of where the room and the readers agreed, and where they didn't.

  • - Current poll: the first poll covers the agenda for the regular meeting expected Monday, November 16, 2026, and will be linked here when it opens the week before the meeting.
  • - Past results: none yet — the November 16, 2026 meeting is the first cycle.

These are reader polls: self-selected, open to anyone, and not scientific polling. They measure who showed up to answer, not what Cabot thinks. They are published so the participation is on the record, not mistaken for it.

Flock cameras and license-plate readers

What it is. Nine Flock Safety license-plate cameras watched Cabot's entrances and corridors from early 2022 under a lease signed in January 2022. In the first half of 2026 the police department reported the system scanned 8.04 million plates, produced 688 hot-list matches, and figured in seven stolen-vehicle recoveries and 19 arrests. On August 6, 2026, Mayor Kincade ordered all nine cameras covered and disconnected, citing privacy concerns from the national debate while saying he did not believe Cabot officers had misused the system. They have been off since.

The nuance. Three different outcomes get blurred into one argument. Suspension already happened — that was the mayor's order. Permanent removal was put to the council on October 19, 2026, after a two-hour public hearing with up to 200 participants, and the resolution failed 2–3–3. A funding ban is a separate instrument: Ordinance No. 33 of 2026, which would bar spending city funds on automated license-plate-reader systems, passed its second reading on November 2, 2026; a motion to skip the third reading and pass it that night failed 4–4 (waiving readings takes six of eight votes), so its third reading and vote are expected at the November 16, 2026 meeting, where passage needs five of eight. "Cameras" also splits in the group conversation: after three months in which the word meant surveillance, an October 24 thread about vandalism at Richie Road park drew heavy demands for ordinary cameras at park facilities — with residents themselves distinguishing park cameras from plate readers.

The arguments. Privacy critics — speakers at the October hearing and the dominant voice in the groups — describe mass surveillance: a private company collecting plate data to cloud servers, shareable with agencies nationwide, building a record of where law-abiding people travel, adopted without any standalone council vote. The cameras' defenders, including retired law-enforcement speakers and three council members who voted against permanent removal, point to the department's own numbers (the scans, matches, recoveries, and arrests above), note that no misuse by a Cabot officer has been alleged anywhere in the record, and argue suspension punished a working tool. A middle position heard in the groups and stated on the council would keep camera capability but end the third-party custody of the data; Council Member Corey Spangler's version of it is to undo the current third-party system first and treat any in-house system as a separate, later public decision under public oversight — he is not proposing a city-run replacement now, and abstained on the removal resolution because he prefers the binding ordinance to a symbolic resolution.

Misinformation check.

  • - "The council voted to install Flock after a public debate." The record shows no standalone adoption vote: the system was approved as line items in the 2024, 2025, and 2026 budgets, a point Council Member Matt Webber made at the August 17, 2026 meeting. The first public hearing came in October 2026, four years in.
  • - "Flock keeps your data for 30 days" / "for 150 days." Both figures are documented — for different buckets. The vendor deletes footage on a rolling 30-day basis under the agreement; plate data the department itself preserves is capped at 150 days by department policy, matching Arkansas Code § 12-12-1804(a)(2). Claims using either number as the whole story are incomplete.
  • - "Cabot officers misused the data." No allegation of local misuse appears in the coverage or the council packet. The misuse examples raised in debate were from other jurisdictions, offered as risk.
  • - "The cameras are gone for good." They are suspended and covered. Permanent removal failed on October 19; the funding ban is still pending. Nothing about the end state has been decided.

Water and sewer bills

What it is. Cabot's water and sewer system is operated by the Cabot Water and Wastewater Commission (Cabot Waterworks); under state law (Ark. Code Ann. § 14-234-214), the rates themselves are fixed by the city council, by ordinance. The charts barely moved for a generation — sewer rates dated to 2001, base water rates to 2005. Then, in two steps: the council adopted new sewer rates on October 6, 2025 (Ordinance No. 28 of 2025), phased in January 1, 2026 and January 1, 2027, taking a typical 5,000-gallon sewer bill from $9.81 to $15.62 a month; and on May 4, 2026 it adopted Ordinance No. 15 of 2026 (passed 8–0), which left the 2005 base water rates untouched and added a purchased-water charge — $2.92 per 1,000 gallons from October 1, 2026, rising on an adopted schedule to $4.91 by 2032. The driver is a state mandate: the Arkansas Natural Resources Commission has ordered Cabot off its groundwater wells by 2026, so essentially all of its water must be bought from Central Arkansas Water at CAW's wholesale rates. The first bills carrying the new charge landed around November 1–2, 2026, and the groups lit up: residents comparing jumps of roughly $25–$40, first-ever bills over $100, and urging attendance at the November 2 council meeting.

The nuance. The November anger was aimed at a council meeting, but the chain has two links — a commission that runs the system and a council that sets rates by ordinance — and the part residents felt first (the purchased-water line item) is a pass-through of another utility's wholesale price, not a raise of Cabot's own 2005 base chart. Impact also varies household to household: the charge is per gallon used, and in the same November threads some residents reported no change at all. And one comparison cuts against the outrage: even after its full increase, Cabot's 5,000-gallon sewer bill ($15.62) sat far below the 2025 figures for Beebe ($31.50), Jacksonville ($33.61), Ward ($37.92), and Austin ($42.46).

The arguments. Residents absorbing the new bills talk about affordability, shutoffs, bills that seem to arrive too often, and water quality — extreme hardness damaging appliances and fixtures — and many simply did not know the charge was coming or why. The utility's case is documentary: state law (Act 605 of 2021) requires periodic engineering rate studies, the 2025 Crist Engineers studies concluded the frozen rates could not carry the systems, the wells were ordered closed by the state, and CAW's wholesale schedule rises whether Cabot likes it or not. Transparency critics split the difference: whatever the merits, the charge arrived as an unexplained line item — a proposed ordinance (No. 16 of 2026) that would have required the commission to mail customers a sample bill showing the coming charges was tabled in April 2026.

Misinformation check.

  • - "The council voted this increase in May" (as residents reported being told when they called the water department). Half true and mostly blurred: the May 4, 2026 vote did adopt the purchased-water charge that took effect October 1 — but the sewer increases were voted in October 2025, and neither vote raised the 2005 base water rates, which are still in force.
  • - "The city council has nothing to do with it — it's all the commission." Also circulating, and also wrong: the commission operates the system, but state law leaves rate-fixing to the council by ordinance, and both 2025–26 changes were council ordinances. Both bodies are in the chain.
  • - "An outside firm is behind the bills" (a national asset-management firm was named in one thread). No record reviewed connects any outside firm to Cabot Waterworks' rates. The documented drivers are the state well-shutdown mandate and Central Arkansas Water's wholesale schedule.
  • - "Everyone's bill went up by the same percentage." The record shows a per-gallon charge; the percentage depends on usage and on which chart — the sewer per-gallon charge nearly doubles by 2027, the minimum charge rises about 23 percent, and sewer-only customers' minimums more than double. There is no single percentage.

The Buckman Brewhouse lease

What it is. The city owns the old Central Fire Station at 114 W. Pine Street and leases it to Buckman Brewhouse, a family brewery and restaurant that opened in February 2026 after a long permit fight. The 2024 lease — passed on a mayoral tie-break after a 4–4 council vote — runs 20 years. Instead of cash rent for the first ten years, the tenant must complete at least $600,000 in repairs and improvements at its own expense, all of which become city property; after the first decade, rent is $8,200 a month. The permit fight that followed was about parking (the building has none; the code requires 34 spaces) and about alcohol in dry Lonoke County, where the brewhouse serves under a state private-club license. That license is also why it cannot sell growlers to take home — off-premises sales are barred for dry-county private clubs, and changing that takes an act of the state legislature.

The nuance. The money story depends on which document you read, and this page does not settle it. The Leader's October 28, 2026 survey of city properties describes $5,000 a month paid not to the city but to Southern Bank, holder of the brewhouse's SBA renovation loan, "in lieu of" rent until the loan is repaid. The 2024 lease text contains no bank, no SBA loan, and no $5,000 payment — the improvements are the rent. No lease amendment documenting the change has been found in the council packets in this archive, and the SBA loan's total does not appear in any source reviewed. The $600,000 and $8,200 figures agree across both accounts; the $5,000 stream does not.

The arguments. Supporters — including the mayor, whose tie-breaks carried both the lease and the permit's second chance — see a dead downtown building saved at the tenant's expense, an anchor business, and improvements the city keeps either way. Critics include the four council members who voted against the lease, the planning commissioners who denied the permit 5–0 over parking, the next-door law office that bears the parking and alley burden, and residents who opposed a brewery on dry-county principle near schools and churches. One council critic's position was narrower: for the brewery, against this location.

Misinformation check.

  • - "The city gave the building away." The lease obliges the tenant to spend at least $600,000 improving a city-owned building in the first decade, with quarterly reporting, and the improvements stay with the city; cash rent of $8,200 a month follows. Whether that is a good deal is the argument; that it is no deal is not in the documents.
  • - Any confident statement of what the brewhouse currently pays, and to whom. The 2024 lease and the 2026 newspaper summary describe different payment streams for the renovation years, and the reconciling amendment is not in the record. Treat precise current-rent claims — from any side — as unsettled until the executed lease and amendments are produced.
  • - "The city could just let them sell growlers." The restriction follows from state ABC private-club law in a dry county. It is a Little Rock decision, not a City Hall one.

Go deeper: Buckman Brewhouse — both tie-break votes, the permit appeal, and the figures conflict laid out in full.

Growth pressure: the crypto mine, data centers, and who gets to say no

What it is. In 2024 a cryptocurrency mine was built on county land at 494 Grayhawk Circle — outside Cabot's city limits but inside its planning district, under five miles from Little Rock Air Force Base. Neighbors discovered it rather than being told; the county judge's cease-and-desist (November 27, 2024) was lifted after FEMA issued a floodplain permit; and on February 3, 2025 the council extended its commercial development rules across the planning district (Ordinances No. 8, 9, and 10 of 2025), expressly covering crypto mines and data centers. Five weeks later the Arkansas Senate passed Act 314 of 2025, repealing municipal planning-district authority statewide, effective August 5, 2025. The tool Cabot used no longer exists. A broader data-center argument continues at the state level, where the Arkansas Data Centers Act (Act 851 of 2023) limits how far local governments can single out mining operations, and a 2025 bill to ban mines near military facilities died in committee. One council member has published a drafted — never introduced — city data-center ordinance (size-tiered zoning, noise limits at residential lines, closed-loop cooling, grid-capacity studies) as his platform's answer.

The nuance. At every decisive step, the decision sat somewhere other than Cabot: a federal floodplain permit overruled the county; state law first protected the industry from local rules, then removed the local workaround; the mine's own current status — built, operating, or abandoned — is confirmed by no source in this archive through October 2026, and is labeled a gap rather than guessed at. The company's compliance claims (a self-imposed 55-decibel fence-line limit, no excess water or power, the Air Force aware and not objecting) are the company's claims; the record contains no independent study to test them against.

The arguments. Grayhawk neighbors led with process — construction discovered, not announced; the county judge told it would be a mini-storage facility; studies after the fact — and with the standard impacts: fan noise, grid strain, water use, property values. Local officials were, unusually, one voice: the mayor, the county judge, and both state legislators stood together against it, with the senator adding a national-security case. The company's answer was compliance and calibration (see above). The state-level counter-argument — the one Act 314 embodies — treats extraterritorial planning authority itself as the problem: city rules governing people who cannot vote in the city. Council Member Spangler calls the senator's Act 314 vote, after urging the council to use that very authority in its own chamber, a "betrayal"; that is his characterization, presented as his — the roll call (Senate, 24–5, March 12, 2025, Hill among the yeas) is public record, and Hill was not the bill's sponsor.

Misinformation check.

  • - "The mine is a foreign operation." The owner, Interstate Holdings, is the Landers family's Arkansas firm — auto dealers for decades. Reporting found no basis for the foreign-ownership suspicion aired early in the fight.
  • - "The council could have just stopped it." The county's stop order was lifted on a federal permit; the city attorney cautioned at the time that the city could not stop the project; and the one regulatory tool the council did use was repealed by the state five months later.
  • - "The mine is running today" / "the mine was stopped for good." Neither is documented. Construction was cleared to resume in December 2024 and the company said it would proceed; no source since confirms completion, operation, or abandonment.

City debt, bonds, and what the money built

What it is. Cabot's capital budget runs on a one-cent sales tax first approved in 2005 and extended by voters in 2013 and 2021, and the bonds that tax secures. The August 10, 2021 special election — the current argument's ground zero — authorized a program everyone calls the $69 million bond issue: the fiber network, Game Time at the Grounds ($16 million, opened May 2024), streets and drainage, public-safety facilities, tornado sirens, a wave pool now under construction for summer 2027, and more, alongside refunding the older bonds from 5 percent down to 3 percent. The city's total outstanding bonded debt at the end of 2022 was approximately $99.65 million — all of its bonded debt, much of it backed by dedicated revenue sources rather than the general fund. The administration says the 35-year bonds can be retired by 2038, some 17–18 years early, on the sales tax alone.

The nuance. The argument keeps merging money pots that are legally separate: the sales-tax bonds; franchise-fee revenue bonds (which bought the Price Cutter buildings that became the Justice Center); the Advertising & Promotion tax; and land-sale proceeds. The September 2026 fight over a $50,000 A&P study of a possible $10–15 million amphitheater ran almost entirely on that confusion (see the process section below). The turnout argument is real but routinely misstated: 974 people voted in August 2021 — a landslide among those who did, a few percent of the city's roughly 17,000 registered voters. And the competing payoff claims ("paid off in 2038 even with no growth") are the city's statements, consistent with each other but not independently audited in any source reviewed; the Leader's own news-column assessment was that there is no evidence the bond issues have hurt the city's finances.

The arguments. The debt case, pressed hardest by 2026 mayoral challenger Eddie Joe Williams, is two-layered: the size of the obligation, and consent — in his review of the returns, only about 900 of some 17,000 registered voters cast ballots on the city's largest-ever borrowing. The administration answers: the 2021 vote continued an existing tax rather than levying a new one, refunded old debt at a lower rate, is collateralized by the sales tax instead of general funds, and is ahead of schedule. In the groups, the resident version is less about totals than about priorities — roads and familiar facilities (the summer 2026 campaign to repair the community center's therapy pool ran while a wave-pool design was being solicited) versus new landmarks, and about learning of spending decisions from the newspaper.

Misinformation check.

  • - "The 2021 bond issue put Cabot $100 million in debt." Three numbers are being used as one: $69 million (the program's label), about $103 million (the ballot's combined authorized maximums, including the $24.8 million refunding), and $99.65 million (the city's total bonded debt at the end of 2022, across all issues and pots). Each is documented; they are not interchangeable.
  • - "The 2021 vote raised taxes." It continued the existing one-cent tax (the city's total sales tax is two cents) and refunded older bonds at a lower interest rate.
  • - "Only about 700 people decided it." Close, but rounded down: the Leader counted 974 voting, with the main questions drawing 807, 780, 761, and 628 yes votes. The underlying point — hundreds of yes votes out of some 17,000 registered voters — stands on the reported figures.

Roads, traffic, and growth

What it is. Cabot's defining daily grievance. The city grew from 9,673 people in 1990 to 23,776 in 2010; growth has since steadied (26,569 in the 2020 census, an estimated 27,711 by mid-2025), but the chain development and the road construction landed on the same two corridors at the same time. The big projects: the $32 million North Interchange (opened March 2019; the city contributed $10.1 million from its 2013 bonds), the six-lane widening of Hwy. 67/167 (under construction, expected 2027), and the $53.9 million rebuild of the Interstate 57/Highway 5 interchange as a single-point urban interchange (ARDOT contract, ground broken May 2026, scheduled to finish mid-2028). Highway 5 north of the interstate carries about 18,000 vehicles a day.

The nuance. Most of what residents curse is not the city's to build. Interstate 57 and Highways 5, 38, 89, 321, and 367 are state highways — ARDOT designs, funds, and schedules them, including both interchange rebuilds and the roundabouts. The city's levers are its own streets and drainage, its land-use and incentive decisions along the corridors, and its ability to lobby and partner. The group conversation rarely draws that line; the record does.

The arguments. One camp grades the mayor for all of it — the single most-engaged traffic thread found (BCOC, August 6, 2026) blamed him personally for interchange spending and cross-town delay. The pushback in the same thread split three ways: the problems predate him by decades; at least he is doing something and these things take time; and the premise itself was wrong on the day (the project had just started, the money was federal and state, and an unusually large funeral explained part of the backup). Underneath sits the twin reflex of the growth argument — "towns that don't grow, die" against "if people would quit moving here" — while the same groups that curse the traffic turn out in force for new chain restaurants. The daily flashpoints are specific rather than systemic: the high-school pickup line, school-zone speeding, drainage ditches, and roundabouts, which split residents between obvious improvement and local nightmare.

Misinformation check.

  • - "The $69 million interchange." No Cabot road project costs $69 million. That figure is the 2021 bond program's total (see the debt section), most of which was not roads. The North Interchange cost $32 million; the Hwy. 5 SPUI contract is $53.9 million.
  • - "The city is building the Highway 5 interchange." It is an ARDOT project, state-designed and state-contracted. The city's documented roles are advocacy, partnership, and its own street network — credit and blame aimed at City Hall for the SPUI's pace are aimed at the wrong government.

The city fiber network

What it is. In the same 2021 special election, voters authorized up to $23.83 million for the city to build its own fiber-optic network as a public utility and lease it to an operator — the first arrangement of its kind in Arkansas. The city chose Connect2First, a First Electric Cooperative subsidiary, over Ritter Communications and Comcast, and leased the lines for 30 years at an initial $35 per subscriber per month, adjusted every two years. Connections began in early 2023; the Leader reported 600 residents connected by November 2023 and 2,000 signed up by July 2024. The monopoly the vote targeted is gone: by 2026 RightFiber, Xfinity, Brightspeed, and wireless carriers all sell home internet in Cabot alongside Connect2First.

The nuance. Three cost figures attach to the project and are routinely merged: $23.83 million (the ballot's authorized maximum), $22 million (the city's program estimate), and $20 million (the build figure in city and vendor announcements). No audited final construction cost appears in the record. And the economics have a documented hole: no Cabot-specific subscriber count has been published since July 2024, so the city's per-subscriber lease income — the number that would show whether the network pays its way — cannot be estimated from public sources. The same gap applies to the Connect2First office lease in the Justice Center, where the total back rent being repaid at $6,500 a month is stated in no source reviewed.

The arguments. Supporters start from the grievance that produced the vote — a decade of cable bills, speeds far below advertised plans, and cancellation ordeals — and point to the service legend of the early years (a cut line repaired in about ninety minutes, an office in town, technicians who come out) and a state planning award. Skeptics and the merely tired point to the build-out's waits and, by 2026, an incumbent's complaint profile: outages, equipment confusion, and neighbors quietly leaving for wireless — the grievance redistributed among five or six providers rather than solved once for all.

Misinformation check.

  • - "The fiber network cost $23.83 million." That is the authorized ceiling, not a cost figure; the build number the city and vendors use is $20 million, and no audited final cost is in the record.
  • - "Connect2First has 34,000 customers in Cabot." The 34,000 figure (fall 2025) is the company's entire Arkansas system. The last Cabot-specific number reported is 2,000 sign-ups in July 2024.

How City Hall decides: process and transparency

What it is. Several of Cabot's loudest fights are not about what was decided but about how. Three exhibits run through this page. The Flock system arrived as budget line items, with no standalone vote — for process critics, the problem in itself. The amphitheater episode (September 2026) showed the information gap from the other side: a $50,000 study, funded by the Advertising & Promotion Commission for a possible $10–15 million amphitheater, drew one of the year's largest group threads, argued almost entirely as if it were bond money — until the original poster publicly corrected the record three days later and sorted the pots. And the three-readings fight (fall 2026) put the machinery itself on the agenda: Arkansas law requires an ordinance to be read on three separate days before passage, unless two-thirds of all council members vote to suspend the rule; on November 2, 2026, the motion to waive Ordinance 33's third reading and pass the Flock funding ban before the election failed 4–4 — it needed six of eight — so the decision waits for the regular timetable, where passage needs a simple majority of the whole council, five of eight.

The nuance. The same machinery cuts both ways, and the record shows both: in December 2024, Cabot's own state senator urged the council to pass the planning-district ordinance as an emergency, suspending the rules — the council instead adopted it on the regular timetable two months later. Meeting access has its own paper trail: livestreaming of council meetings was formally authorized by Resolution No. 49 of 2024, co-sponsored by Council Member Spangler and passed unanimously (8–0) on October 21, 2024. Against that, the minutes of the October 19, 2026 regular meeting — the Flock hearing night — are missing from the city's posted records, a gap this archive shares and names rather than papers over. And the groups have become the de facto bulletin board: hearing notices circulated there precisely because, as one October post put it in paraphrase, very few people read the newspaper anymore.

The arguments. The deliberation case: big decisions should sit in public view across multiple meetings, with readings, hearings, and agendas people can actually follow — speed is how things like a surveillance network arrive unexamined. The speed case: emergencies and deadlines are real, the waiver exists in state law for a reason, and a determined majority delayed is a decision made by procedure instead of by vote. On information, critics across fights converge on one complaint — residents learn what their government is doing late, from the newspaper or the groups, rather than from the city — while the formal record (packets, livestreams, published agendas) is more available than at any point in the city's history.

Misinformation check.

  • - "Councilman Spangler changed the rules to require three readings and raised the waiver threshold." He did not. The three-reading requirement and the two-thirds waiver standard are Arkansas law — Ark. Code § 14-55-202(a), on the books since Acts 1875 — restated in Cabot's council rules since at least January 1, 2023, before his authorship, and carried forward unchanged in his Ordinance No. 2 of 2025. His actual 2025 rules change was narrower and in the same spirit: §5 (Agendas) now sends a committee item approved at a Work Session onto a Regular Session agenda on a subsequent date, with same-date consideration only by majority vote — before, an item could reach the council the same night it cleared committee.
  • - "If the council wants something, it can pass it in one night." Only by waiving the readings, which takes six of eight votes. The November 2 waiver failed 4–4; that is why Ordinance 33's vote falls on November 16.
  • - "The amphitheater study spends bond money." The $50,000 study is Advertising & Promotion Commission money — a separate tax pot, not the sales-tax bonds and not the council's to spend. This was the subject of the original poster's own public correction in September 2026.

How this page is kept

Issues are added when the record supports them — council action, sustained group discussion, or both — and sections are revised as the record changes (a vote taken, a figure settled, a gap filled). Claims in the misinformation checks are drawn from the discussion itself; where a claim could not be reproduced in the sources, it is not carried here. If you have a document that settles something this page leaves open — the Buckman lease amendment, the Grayhawk mine's operating status, a Cabot-specific fiber subscriber count — the Contribute page is the way to put it in the archive.
