The Amphitheater Fight of 2026

In September 2026, the Cabot Advertising and Promotion Commission agreed to pay $50,000 for a study of whether Cabot could support an amphitheater — a venue that might cost $10 to $15 million to build. The study itself was small, and it committed the city to nothing beyond the analysis. But the announcement, carried on the front page of the Arkansas Leader on September 16, 2026, landed in the middle of a five-year-old argument about debt, taxes, and who decides what Cabot builds next. Within a week it had produced one of the largest Facebook threads of the year in the local groups — and then something rarer: a public correction, after it became clear that the money in question belonged to a different pot than the one most residents were arguing about.

The story

The commission and its money. The Cabot Advertising and Promotion (A&P) Commission is not the city council, and its money is not the city's general budget. The city created the commission in 1993. Its revenues come from a dedicated tax — 1.5 percent on restaurants and prepared food, and 3 percent on hotels — and its stated mission is to spend those receipts on tourism promotion and on the growth of the city's Parks and Recreation Department. Under Arkansas law and in the city's own audited reporting, A&P funds are a separate reporting entity: the commission holds and spends them for advertising, promotion, and parks purposes. Commissioners, by the Leader's account of a January 2026 vacancy, are drawn from owners and managers of tourism businesses such as hotels, motels, and restaurants. The commission's minutes do not appear as council business; they surface, when they surface, as exhibits inside council packets — one reason its decisions can arrive in public view without the run-up residents expect from council items.

The commission in action: the wave pool. The A&P Commission had already made one large commitment in 2026. In January, it approved $900,000 toward the wave pool at the Cabot Aquatic Park, payable at $15,000 a month over five years and carried in its 2026 budget. The wave pool itself is a Parks and Recreation project from the 2021 bond program — voters approved it as part of the parks share of that issue — but its projected cost had climbed from $1.9 million (the 2020 projection) to about $2.8 million, and the A&P contribution came on top of the bond money. The episode established the pattern the amphitheater would follow: a parks-and-entertainment project, funded by braiding together pots of money that residents often experience as a single tax bill.

The study. Discussion of a possible amphitheater began in 2025, according to the Leader's account. In September 2026 the commission approved paying $50,000 to Sports Facilities Companies, a business management consultant based in Clearwater, Florida, to determine the market for an amphitheater in Cabot and the size it should be. The Leader put the story on its front page on September 16, 2026, under the question of whether Cabot could support such a venue.

What the commission had in mind, as city officials described it to the paper, was an outdoor venue with fixed seats, lawn seating, and permanent restrooms, on part of the roughly 400 acres the city owns near Game Time at the Grounds and the Lyon College School of Veterinary Medicine, off Ernie Davis Drive near I-57 — though no location had been chosen. The estimate for building it was $10 to $15 million. The venue could be rented out for community events, and sponsorships might help cover maintenance and operations.

The officials who spoke for the idea framed it as an extension of what Cabot already does. The city's special projects director described Cabot as a sports-tourism community and an amphitheater as a different element of the same draw — one that, in his framing, would have to make fiscal sense and pay off in economic activity. The city's economic development director pointed to the festivals: CabotFest and the Cabot Strawberry Festival are held on downtown streets, CabotFest draws on the order of 30,000 people, the events have outgrown the space, the carnival ends up in a parking lot, and downtown businesses feel the squeeze. A permanent venue, she argued, would give the festivals room, flexibility, and the ability to book bigger acts and add events. The same account carried the caution: moving festivals off downtown streets has mixed results elsewhere — Beebe's fall festival declined after moving to a ballpark and returned downtown under a new name, and Jacksonville's festivals left Dupree Park for West Main Street in 2023.

What the study does and doesn't commit. The $50,000 bought analysis, not a building. There is no design, no chosen site, and no construction funding. The Leader's account was explicit about the only funding path anyone described: to build the amphitheater, the commission would need to propose an additional A&P tax of one percent; that proposal would have to go before the city council for approval to place it on a ballot; and the voters would decide. None of those steps had happened when the story ran. The study's job was to say whether the market justified even asking.

The older venue context. Cabot's big gatherings have historically been downtown street affairs. CabotFest — founded in 1978, in the rebuilding years after the 1976 tornado (see the tornado page) — and the Strawberry Festival close streets around the City Plaza area rather than filling a venue built for them; the city's post-tornado civic core was built for government, not audiences. That history cuts both ways in the amphitheater debate: the festivals are the strongest argument that Cabot can draw a crowd, and the strongest evidence that the city has never had a permanent place to put one.

Timeline

  • - 1993 — The City of Cabot creates the Advertising and Promotion Commission to manage the prepared-food and hotel tax receipts.
  • - August 10, 2021 — Voters approve the bond program whose parks share includes the wave pool (projected at $1.9 million in 2020).
  • - 2025 — Discussion of a future amphitheater begins, per the Leader's later account.
  • - January 2026 — The A&P Commission approves $900,000 toward the wave pool — $15,000 a month for five years — in its 2026 budget.
  • - September 2026 — The A&P Commission approves $50,000 to Sports Facilities Companies for a market-and-size study of an amphitheater estimated at $10–15 million. The Leader reports it on the front page on September 16.
  • - September 22, 2026 — A campaign post by Council Member Kyle Pickard in the Cabot Strong Facebook group attacks the study and the tax discussion; the thread draws 135 reactions and 132 comments.
  • - September 25, 2026 — Pickard publishes a follow-up post correcting the tax at issue (67 reactions, 49 comments).
  • - September 23 – October 28, 2026 — The Leader's subsequent issues carry no follow-up on the study: no results, no site, no tax proposal.

What critics said

The opposition had two layers. The first was elected-official and campaign-season criticism: the study was wasteful spending on a project residents had not asked for, and any road to building it ran through higher taxes. The second layer was the groups, where the amphitheater became a vessel for the whole post-2021 argument about the bond program — the debt total, the taxes that never seem to end, and the suspicion that residents learn what their government is doing from the newspaper after the fact.

The Facebook discourse

The fight played out mainly in Cabot Strong, the largest of the local groups; searches of Cabot's Petty Citizens – BCOC, WTF Cabot, and the smaller Cabot groups found no comparable amphitheater thread. On the evening of Tuesday, September 22, 2026 — six days after the Leader story, and six weeks before the November 3 election — Kyle Pickard, the Ward 4, Position 2 council member seeking re-election, posted about the study as a campaign statement. The post pointed readers to the Leader's story, said the project could cost $10 to $15 million, and framed the funding discussion as the city possibly raising sales taxes: Cabot's combined state, county, and city sales tax rate stood at roughly 10 percent, the post said, and another percent would take it to about 11. The post pledged opposition to raising taxes for the project and closed with a campaign appeal. It drew 135 reactions and 132 comments — among the largest local-government threads of the year in the groups.

The comments reprised, nearly complete, the argument residents have been having since the 2021 bond election:

  • - Debt first. Commenters returned repeatedly to the city's roughly $100 million in total bonded debt, arguing the existing one-cent sales tax should go entirely to paying bonds off before anything new is studied, and that a city carrying that debt should not be building more things that cost more money.
  • - Project taxes never end. A recurring claim, in the bond debates since 2021 and again here, was that taxes sold as paying for a project do not retire when the project is done — they hang around, or get reallocated to something else.
  • - Roads and basics first. Roads, infrastructure, and crime came before entertainment; Cabot was described as a bedroom community, not an entertainment community, and an amphitheater as something other places have. Several commenters doubted any amphitheater but the Walmart venue in Rogers succeeds, and noted the dry-county fit. Others asked why $50,000 was needed just to explore the idea, suggested private donors instead, and pointed to unfinished business — the wave pool still not built, ballfield scoreboards, parking at the soccer fields.
  • - Learning it from the newspaper. One commenter put the transparency complaint directly to the mayor inside the thread: why were residents finding out about the spending through the newspaper instead of a council meeting, and why was money going to an out-of-state firm? Another noted, from the other direction, that the item had never appeared in a city council packet. Both observations were accurate about the structure, if not the intent: the A&P Commission is a separate body from the council, its approvals are not council agenda items, and the Leader's front page was, in practice, the public announcement.
  • - The shooting-range comparison. As in other threads this year, a commenter reached for the police indoor shooting range — a taxpayer-funded facility residents themselves cannot use — as the standing example of building things the public pays for but does not get to use.
  • - An older amphitheater. One commenter recalled that an amphitheater idea dated back to Mayor Bill Cypert's tenure and that it had once been imagined for roughly where Game Time now stands. That recollection is unverified in the documents reviewed — nothing in the Leader or council corpus confirms an earlier city amphitheater proposal — but it suggests the idea circulating in 2026 was older than the study.

Inside the same thread, other commenters pushed back on the premise — and this pushback is where the correction began. Several explained that the study was the A&P Commission's, paid from the commission's own allotted budget rather than by city order; that A&P funds are completely separate from the city's accounts, money the city cannot spend or access; and that the tax under discussion was the existing restaurant tax structure — the 1.5 percent on prepared food — not the general sales tax. Support for the venue itself, as distinct from support for the process point, stayed thin in both threads: a handful of commenters called smaller venues a real draw and the study worth looking into, and one framed the fiscal choice as paying more in property taxes or acquiring more revenue sources. The pro-amphitheater case in the public record rests mainly with the officials who made it in the Leader.

The correction. On Friday morning, September 25, 2026 — three days after the original post — Pickard published a follow-up in the same group. It opened by owning the mistake outright: the earlier post had said the amphitheater could affect readers' sales tax, and that was incorrect. The discussion, the correction said, concerned a possible increase in the A&P tax — the tax collected on prepared food and restaurant sales — which is separate from the roughly 10 percent combined general sales tax rate. The post restated the facts its author said remained: the $50,000 study was approved; the project could cost $10 to $15 million; an A&P increase had been discussed as a funding route; and any new tax would have to follow the legal process, including council consideration and, if required, voter approval. It also held the author's underlying position: continued concern about spending $50,000 to study a project that could ultimately require additional taxes, and opposition to increasing the tax burden whatever the tax is called. The post noted the author could simply have edited the original and said nothing, and chose to correct it publicly instead. It drew 67 reactions and 49 comments.

The response to the correction was its own small study in how the argument works. Some commenters thanked Pickard for it directly, and several used the thread to ask that every candidate for council state a position on the amphitheater. But many commenters answered that the correction changed the label, not the burden: a tax is a tax however it is collected; an A&P increase still lands on the diner at the register; restaurants already absorb the cost and penalty risk of collecting it; the A&P tax already sits at 1.5 percent; and one commenter calculated the total tax on a Cabot restaurant check at 11.5 percent and opposed raising it. A few compared Cabot's rates with the roughly 9.5 percent they said neighboring cities charge and warned that higher restaurant taxes push diners to other towns. One commenter worried that any tax question would be scheduled for a low-turnout special election — a concern with a long local pedigree, since the bond program itself was approved in an August 2021 special election — and another raised the traffic question: whether Cabot's roads could handle steady event traffic unless the venue went out by the Grounds and the veterinary college. The distinction between the pots was, by the end of the week, widely understood in the thread. It had not settled anything.

What the record shows

  • - The study approval is documented. The A&P Commission agreed to pay $50,000 to Sports Facilities Companies of Clearwater, Florida, to determine the market and size of a possible amphitheater; discussion of the idea began in 2025; the construction estimate in circulation was $10–15 million; no location had been chosen, with city-owned land near Game Time at the Grounds discussed as a possibility (Leader, Sept. 16, 2026).
  • - The money pots really are separate — in both directions. A&P revenues (1.5 percent on prepared food and restaurant receipts, 3 percent on hotels) are the commission's, dedicated by law to advertising and promotion and to parks and recreation, and are treated as a separate reporting entity in the city's audited statements; the city council does not appropriate them, and the $50,000 did not come from the general fund or the bond program. By the same token, A&P money is not a general-purpose fund: it could not simply be redirected to roads or bond payoff, which is the use several commenters proposed for it.
  • - No tax had been proposed, let alone approved. The only funding path described in the record for construction — an additional one-percent A&P tax — would require the commission to propose it, the council to approve placing it on a ballot, and the voters to pass it. As of October 28, 2026, none of those steps appears in the record. The original Facebook post's framing of the funding discussion as a possible general sales-tax increase was, as its author corrected three days later, not the tax under discussion.
  • - The wave-pool precedent is documented. In January 2026 the commission approved $900,000 toward the roughly $2.8 million wave pool at the Cabot Aquatic Park — $15,000 a month for five years, in its 2026 budget — layered on top of the wave pool's 2021 bond funding, whose 2020 projection had been $1.9 million (Leader, Jan. 21 and Oct. 28, 2026).
  • - The commission's structure explains the surprise. The A&P Commission was created by the city in 1993; its members are tourism-industry owners and managers; its business is not transacted at council meetings, and its minutes reach the public mainly as exhibits in council packets. A resident who follows the council closely could still first meet an A&P decision in the newspaper — as, in September 2026, most of Cabot did.
  • - Attendance claims are officials' estimates. CabotFest's draw of roughly 30,000 people comes from the city's economic development director in the Leader's account; no independent count was found. The claim that neighboring cities' combined sales tax rate is about 9.5 percent comes from commenters, not from any source reviewed, and is not verified here.
  • - Gaps. The record reviewed does not include: the exact date of the commission's September 2026 study vote or its minutes (A&P minutes were not in the council packets in this corpus); the study's scope, deliverable, or due date; any result or interim finding from Sports Facilities Companies; the commission's annual A&P revenue or fund balance, against which the $50,000 could be weighed; the tax base an additional one-percent A&P levy would apply to; any site-selection work; an operating model (city-run, commission-run, or private operator); or any documentation of the earlier, Cypert-era amphitheater idea recalled in the Facebook thread. As of the Leader's October 28, 2026 issue — the last in the record — the study's status is simply that it was commissioned; nothing further had been reported.
