What the Bond Money Built

The story

For two decades, Cabot has run its capital budget on a single engine: a one-cent sales tax that voters keep agreeing to continue, and the bonds that tax secures. The tax was first approved in 2005 and extended by voters in 2013 and again in 2021 — each time as a continuation, not a new tax (the city's total sales tax rate is two cents). Every few years the city has taken the same bargain back to the ballot: keep the penny, and in exchange get a list of buildings, trucks, pipes and parks, plus a refinancing of the older bonds at whatever interest rate the moment offers. The 2021 round was the biggest yet, and it is the round the 2026 mayoral election is being fought over — because it produced both the most visible building boom in the city's history and the largest debt figure ever attached to it.

The earlier rounds (2005–2020). The 2005 approval started the cycle; the 2013 extension came with a bond series that both refunded earlier bonds and financed new improvements, and a further improvement series followed in 2016 (the Series 2013 and Series 2016 bonds are named in the 2021 refunding ordinance). The best-documented project of the 2013 round is the North Interchange: the state awarded Manhattan Road and Bridge Co. a $25.5 million contract for the Hwy. 67/167–Hwy. 38 interchange north of town, and Cabot paid roughly $10 million of it from its 2013 bond issue — $10.1 million in the Leader's year-end accounting (Dec. 25, 2019) — toward a project whose total cost the city puts at $32 million. The interchange opened in March 2019 (see the Growth & Traffic page). By late 2020 the council was already discussing refinancing the 2013 bonds as rates fell (council agenda, Dec. 14, 2020).

The 2021 special election. The city took the next round to voters in a special election on August 10, 2021, after a summer of town-hall meetings (July 8 and 15, 2021) and a campaign run through a dedicated site, cabotbond.com. The ballot was not one question but a series of them, each authorizing bonds up to a stated maximum (Ordinance No. 12 of 2021, in the Oct. 4, 2021 council packet):

  • - $24,835,000 to refund the outstanding Series 2013 and Series 2016 bonds at lower rates;
  • - $23,830,000 for broadband/telecommunications infrastructure;
  • - $10,835,000 for street improvements and $10,835,000 for drainage and flood control;
  • - $3,255,000 for police and $2,605,000 for fire — training facilities, a substation, vehicles and equipment in each case;
  • - $275,000 for early-warning (tornado siren) facilities;
  • - $4,230,000 for animal services facilities;
  • - $21,775,000 for parks and recreation — a multi-purpose community center with basketball and volleyball courts, a wave pool at the aquatic park, artificial turf for baseball fields, a playground, a community pavilion;
  • - $545,000 for a city food pantry, to be operated by a nonprofit.

The Leader reported 974 people voting, in the middle of a pandemic summer, at the public library and the community center. Every question passed: the refunding and extension questions by roughly four to one (807–166 and 780–194), parks by 761–214, and the closest question by 628–347 (Leader, Aug. 11, 2021). Voters also allowed the refunding itself, which cut the rate on the older bonds from 5 percent to 3 percent while the city took on the new debt at the lower rate. The package was universally described — by the city, the Leader, and both 2026 campaigns — as a $69 million bond issue, though the ballot's separate maximums, refunding included, summed to more (about $103 million in authorizations; see "What the record shows"). The city sold the bonds, hired a special projects director to oversee the work (former public works director Brian Boroughs, Aug. 2021), and started projects in January 2022.

What got built, in the order it landed. The January 5, 2022 Leader laid out the approved program with the city's own estimates: $22 million for citywide fiber-optic broadband; $21.7 million for Parks & Rec; $21.6 million for streets and drainage; $6 million for public safety (a shared fire and police substation and training center with an indoor shooting range and a fire training burn tower, a new pumper truck, police radios, laptops and vehicle cameras); an animal support services and education center with a wellness and surgery center, estimated at $4.2 million, in the old Price Cutter building; and a community pantry of more than 4,000 square feet in the rest of that building, projected at $545,000. Then the delivery record, as the Leader documented it:

  • - Tornado sirens (2021–22). The council approved buying 12 new tornado sirens from Table Rock Alerting Systems on the lowest bid of $299,995 (Leader, Jan. 5, 2022).
  • - The Justice Center and the Price Cutter buildings (2021–2025). In September 2021 the city bought the old Price Cutter building, its parking lot and the old Sears Hometown Store building for $2.9 million — paid for with franchise fee revenue bonds, a separate pot from the sales-tax bonds. The buildings became the Cabot Justice Center (805 W. Main St.), housing the police department, the district courts and city offices, with the community pantry and a Connect2First fiber office in the complex. The Justice Center's construction was funded with special-election bond money and franchise fee revenue bonds (Leader, Oct. 28, 2026). Inside it, the state-required $4.5 million Lonoke County Emergency Communication Center went online in April 2025 — $1.88 million in construction and $2.67 million in equipment and antenna tower, with the county and the county's cities sharing the cost alongside nearly $1 million from the state.
  • - City fiber (2022– ). In March 2022 the council approved an agreement with Connect2First (a First Electric Cooperative subsidiary) after a bidding process: the city would build the fiber network in its rights-of-way and lease the lines to Connect2First for 30 years, at an initial $35 per subscriber per month, adjusted every two years. By November 2023 the Leader reported 600 residents connected and 150 waiting, described Cabot as the first city in Arkansas to build its own internet infrastructure as a public utility, and noted the city's fiber plan had won a state planning award. Connect2First also leases office space at the Justice Center (rent $11,000 a month, per the Oct. 28, 2026 Leader).
  • - The fire truck (2023). In October 2023 the Fire Department held a ceremonial push-in for a new $900,000 Rosenbauer heavy rescue/pumper truck at Station 4 on Hwy. 321, purchased with money from the 2021 bond issue (Leader, Oct. 4, 2023).
  • - Game Time at the Grounds (opened May 2024). The parks centerpiece: a $16 million, 128,000-square-foot indoor sports complex on the city's 220-acre Grounds tract off Willie Ray Drive — six multi-use basketball courts convertible to 12 volleyball courts, plus two indoor turf fields — built by Nabholz Construction and designed by Halff Engineering. The ribbon was cut in the first week of May 2024 (Leader, Jan. 17 and May 8, 2024). It is the largest single item in Parks & Rec's $21.7 million share.
  • - The animal services project that moved twice (2022–2025). The 2021 plan put the animal center in the Price Cutter conversion (the combined conversion was priced at $4.7 million in July 2022, covering the animal center, pantry and offices). By August 2023 the design had become a standalone $4.7 million Cabot Animal Support Services facility on 13.4 acres at Kerr Station Road and Adams Street. That building was then dropped in favor of co-locating animal services inside the Lyon College veterinary school complex (see the Lyon College page) — and the Kerr Station land was instead used for the fire department's training tower and the police department's indoor shooting range. The range thus appears in two pots at two times: it was part of the 2021 public-safety package as planned, and in November 2025 the council directed part of the Lyon land-sale proceeds toward it — $2.5 million plus $222,400 in engineering (Leader, Nov. 12, 2025; Oct. 28, 2026). What happens to the 2021 animal-services bond money itself was still open in the Oct. 28, 2026 Leader: it may be refunded or used for a different animal services project, such as renovating the old shelter or building a dog park, but it cannot be spent on other city projects.
  • - The wave pool (2026–27). The last big parks item is still coming: construction is underway on a $2.8 million wave pool expansion at the Cabot Aquatic Park, built by Clarity Pools of Little Rock and set to open in summer 2027, roughly doubling the water park's capacity. Its 2020 projected cost was $1.9 million; the Leader attributes the increase to inflation. The Advertising & Promotion Commission — a separate tax pot — approved $900,000 toward it in January 2026, payable at $15,000 a month for five years (Leader, Oct. 28, 2026).

The 2026 argument. The program's balance sheet became the central issue of the mayoral race. The Leader (Oct. 28, 2026) put the city's total outstanding bonded debt at the end of 2022 at approximately $99.65 million — all of the city's bonded debt, not the 2021 issue alone — noting that much of it is backed by dedicated revenue sources. Challenger Eddie Joe Williams built his campaign on that figure (see "What critics said"). The administration's answer, in the same coverage: the city funded the projects from the bond sales starting in January 2022, and the 35-year bonds can be paid off in 17 years even if Cabot has no growth; at the Oct. 14, 2026 forum Kincade put the payoff in 2038 — 18 years early — and stressed the bonds are collateralized by the one-cent sales tax rather than general city funds.

What critics said

Williams's charge has two layers, and he states both himself. The first is the number: citing the legislative audit, he says Cabot carries nearly $100 million in debt "hanging over our head," and he contrasts it with his own 2007–2010 tenure, in which — his account — his administration paid cash for a fire station and trucks and left $3–4 million in cash on hand. The second layer is consent, and it is the one he presses hardest. In his statement in the Oct. 28, 2026 Leader, he says that when he reviewed the special-election returns he found that of approximately 17,000 registered voters, only about 900 cast ballots and roughly 700 voted yes — a large majority of those who voted, but, in his framing, a very different story about how many people participated in a decision with long-term financial consequences. He says he is not questioning the result; he says major financial decisions deserve broader public awareness, transparency and participation, and that he fought low-turnout special elections on the same grounds as a state senator.

The administration's answers are on the same front page: the measure was a continuation of an existing tax, not a new one; voters also approved refunding the old bonds from 5 percent down to 3 percent; the debt is collateralized by the sales tax; and the bonds can be retired in 17 years on a 35-year schedule even with no growth (payoff 2038, per the mayor at the forum). The Leader's own assessment, in its news columns, was that Williams portrays the debt as bad debt, but there is no evidence the bond issues have hurt the city's finances — and that it is unclear whether Williams publicly opposed the 2021 bond issue before that election.

Process criticism beyond the campaign has mostly been about the mechanism rather than any single project: the use of a stand-alone August special election, called by the council and decided by whoever shows up, for the city's largest-ever borrowing. That criticism predates Williams's candidacy — it was audible in the 2021 group threads themselves (below) — and it is the strand of the argument that survives regardless of what one thinks of any particular building.

The Facebook discourse

The groups hold the whole argument in miniature, across five years, and — unusually — they hold the 2021 debate too, because Cabot Strong's threads from that summer are still readable.

The election itself (summer 2021). Residents used the group as the town hall after the town halls. After a July 15, 2021 bond meeting, one resident posted a long report of questions asked and answered (47 reactions, 83 comments) and a follow-up after speaking with the city treasurer about what would happen to the revenues the projects generated. The comments are the campaign in residents' own terms: procedural questions (who is eligible to vote; whether voting against the extension still lets you vote on the projects); homework questions (whether federal CARES Act money could cover items like the pantry or the sirens instead of borrowing); priorities (firefighter starting pay raised as a competing claim on the same dollars); and the fiber project as the package's most praised item — breaking the incumbent cable provider's monopoly was a live grievance, and the internet project drew the thread's warmest support. The skepticism was just as specific: one commenter relayed the mayor's own figures from the meeting — roughly $25–27 million still owed on the existing bonds, about $103 million with everything on the wish list added — and the frames ranged from "tax and spend" to doubts that earlier bond money had produced much beyond a fire station, to resistance aimed at the animal shelter project's size in particular. A separate August 5, 2021 post made the yes case in plain terms — the tax continues either way for already-approved projects; a yes refinances at lower rates and adds the new list (31 reactions, 66 comments) — and a June 2021 post had already put the opposition's core suspicion in scare quotes: not a tax increase, a tax "extension." A civic-duty strain ran through both threads: read the ballot, vote, and don't complain if you didn't.

The build-out years (2023–2025). Once things started opening, the discourse turned from whether to what it's like to use. The Game Time announcement post in November 2023 (207 reactions, 71 comments) and the May 2024 opening drew the pride register — residents calling the facility gorgeous and impressive, a tournament director's gold mine — with the counterpoint attached in the same threads, sometimes in capital letters: fix the streets, fix the traffic, this will make traffic worse. Everyday friction followed: a June 2024 thread from grandparents objecting that the arena allows no sideline chairs for older or disabled spectators; an October 2024 thread (73 reactions, 152 comments) objecting that the arena takes cards only and turns away cash; questions about memberships versus day passes and about parking and pickup congestion at the complex. And the maintenance counter-theme appeared: residents asking what happens to the older ballpark, and noting the existing rec center gym is crowded with aging equipment — the new flagship measured against the upkeep of what the city already had.

The argument's current form (2026). The debt argument resurfaced in the groups in September 2026 wearing a new costume: a Cabot Strong post (135 reactions, 132 comments) attacking the A&P Commission's $50,000 study of a possible $10–15 million amphitheater. The comments reprised every theme of 2021 — there is already too much bond debt and the full one-cent tax should go to paying it off; project taxes never really end, they hang around or get reallocated; roads should come first; why are residents learning about spending from the newspaper — alongside factual pushback from other commenters that A&P money is a separate pot the city cannot spend, a correction the original poster publicly owned in a follow-up post three days later. One commenter reached for the police indoor shooting range as the standing example of a taxpayer-funded facility residents themselves cannot use. Meanwhile the maintenance-vs-new-build tension organized itself: through July and August 2026, residents ran a small campaign in Cabot Strong around repairing the community center's therapy pool — posts ahead of the council's August 17 vote urging people to contact council members, and a follow-up asking how the vote went — at the same moment Parks & Rec was soliciting design wishes for the wave pool (a February 2026 post drew 94 reactions and 82 comments). The through-line of the discourse is not anti-spending: it is a running negotiation over which spending counts — new landmarks versus the repair of familiar ones, and who gets to use what the money built.

What the record shows

  • - The tax and the totals check out. The one-cent sales tax was approved in 2005 and extended in 2013 and 2021; Cabot's total rate is two cents (Leader, Aug. 4, 2021 and Oct. 28, 2026). The city's total outstanding bonded debt at the end of 2022 was approximately $99.65 million — Williams's "nearly $100 million" is accurate as a total, with the Leader's caveat that much of it is backed by dedicated revenue sources. That total is all city bonded debt (including, for example, the franchise fee revenue bonds behind the Price Cutter purchase), not the 2021 issue alone.
  • - The turnout point is real, and his numbers are rounded down. The Leader's own report of the Aug. 10, 2021 election counted 974 people voting; the principal questions drew 807, 780, 761 and 628 yes votes. Williams's "about 900 cast ballots, roughly 700 yes" understates the reported yes votes on the main questions but is the right order of magnitude, and his underlying ratio — yes votes in the hundreds against roughly 17,000 registered voters (his figure, from his review of the returns) — is not contradicted by anything in the record reviewed.
  • - "$69 million" is the program's name, not the ballot's arithmetic. The separate questions authorized maximums totaling about $103 million including the $24.8 million refunding — a combined figure a resident reported the mayor himself using at a July 2021 meeting. The Leader and the city describe the issue as $69 million; the sources reviewed do not include the final sold amounts series by series, so this page uses $69 million as the program's accepted label and the ordinance maximums as the authorized ceilings, and flags the difference rather than resolving it.
  • - The inventory, with the pots kept straight. Sales-tax bond money (2021 program): 12 tornado sirens, $299,995 bid (2022); the citywide fiber network (city-built, leased to Connect2First for 30 years; 600 subscribers by Nov. 2023); the $900,000 rescue/pumper truck (2023); Game Time at the Grounds, $16 million, opened May 2024, within Parks & Rec's $21.7 million share; part of the Justice Center's construction funding; the wave pool, $2.8 million, opening summer 2027. Franchise fee revenue bonds (a different pot): the $2.9 million Price Cutter/Sears purchase (Sept. 2021) and part of the Justice Center construction. Cost-shared: the $4.5 million emergency communications center (city, county, the county's cities, and nearly $1 million of state money). Land-sale proceeds (a third pot): $2.5 million plus $222,400 engineering toward the police indoor shooting range (Nov. 2025), and the $700,000 Parks reimbursement in the Lyon deal. A&P tax (a fourth pot): $900,000 toward the wave pool (Jan. 2026). The program's street and drainage authorizations were $10,835,000 each; an early documented item is engineering for the Dakota Drive extension ($118,000, Aug. 2021).
  • - The payoff claims are the city's statements, not audited findings. "Pay off the 35-year bonds in 17 years even with no growth" (city, via the Leader, Oct. 28, 2026) and "paid off in 2038, 18 years early" (Kincade, Oct. 14 forum) are consistent with each other; no source reviewed independently models the schedule. The Leader's assessment — no evidence the bond issues have hurt the city's finances — is the paper's judgment, stated in its news story, and is attributed here as such.
  • - Gaps this page names rather than fills: (1) no consolidated per-project final-cost accounting for the street and drainage program appears in the sources reviewed, so this page cannot say what the $21.6 million street/drainage authorization ultimately bought, item by item; (2) completion dates for the smaller parks items in the 2021 list — the turf infields, the all-inclusive playground and splash pad, the community pavilion — are not stated in the sources reviewed (planning coverage in February 2022 placed the splash pad and playground at the Grounds); (3) the shooting range's total final cost and the split between bond money and land-sale proceeds across its changed plans are not stated in one place in the record; (4) the 2021 animal-services bond money's final disposition (refund vs. a different animal project) was still open as of Oct. 28, 2026; (5) the 2005 round's project inventory is not in the sources reviewed — the cycle's first decade is documented here only through the bonds it left behind to be refunded.
